# Hiring, Firing, and Who You Tolerate

Over the years, I've come to believe something that took me a long time
to fully understand: **your culture is not defined by who you celebrate
— it's defined by who you tolerate.**

You can put values on the wall. You can talk about excellence,
ownership, and respect in every all-hands. But the moment your team
sees you look the other way when someone violates those values, the
values stop meaning anything. The wall just becomes decoration.

This is one of the hardest lessons in leadership, because tolerating
people is often the path of least resistance. Letting go of someone is
uncomfortable. Letting a bad fit continue is easy — until it isn't.

## Hire Slow, Fire Fast — But Most of Us Do the Opposite

There's a classic piece of leadership advice: **hire slow, fire fast.**

In practice, most leaders — myself included, at different points in my
career — do the opposite. We rush hiring because we're under pressure
to fill a seat. And we delay firing because it's hard, emotional, and
feels unkind.

Both mistakes compound.

A bad hire costs more than a vacant seat. Much more.

Consider what actually happens when you keep someone who isn't working
out:

- The team around them has to compensate for the gaps.
- Your best people start wondering why you allow it.
- You spend disproportionate energy managing the situation.
- And slowly, your standards drift — not because anyone decided to
  lower them, but because the presence of that person becomes the new
  baseline.

Meanwhile, a vacant seat is just a vacant seat. Uncomfortable, but not
destructive.

## The Person You Kept Too Long

Almost every leader I respect has a story about someone they kept too
long.

I have one too.

You convince yourself that things will turn around. That the next
quarter, the next project, the next conversation will be the one that
works. You invest in them, coach them, give them another chance. And
sometimes that's the right call — people can grow, recover, adapt.

But sometimes you're postponing a decision you've already made
privately. And in the gap between knowing and acting, the cost piles
up silently.

The cost isn't only the person's output. It's the message you're
sending to everyone else.

## Your Best People Are Watching

This is something it took me a while to learn: your best performers
notice when you tolerate poor performance. They notice more than
anyone.

They notice because they hold themselves to a high standard, and when
they see someone else not held to the same standard, it erodes their
sense of fairness. They rarely say anything directly. They just
quietly start wondering whether this is the right place to do their
best work.

Your A-players will almost never tell you they're leaving because of
your B-players. But it's often exactly what happens.

## Hiring: Character Over Comfort

When it comes to hiring, I've noticed something uncomfortable about
the phrase "culture fit" — it often really means *comfort*. Someone
who will agree with us. Someone who won't rock the boat. Someone we
can easily get along with.

That kind of hiring builds teams that stagnate. Everyone thinks the
same way. Everyone reaches for the same solutions. The team loses the
friction that generates new ideas.

I've come to believe that diversity of character is essential — not
just diversity of background or skills, but of temperament, style, and
perspective. The team should include people who challenge each other,
who see the world differently, who push back when you're wrong.

The best hires I've made are often the ones who intimidated me a
little. Someone who knew things I didn't. Someone who, on a given
topic, was clearly sharper than me. Hiring those people isn't a
threat — it's a multiplier.

If you're only comfortable hiring people you can clearly outperform,
you're building a ceiling on your team, and the ceiling is you.

## Firing: Honesty Is a Form of Respect

When it comes time to let someone go, there's a temptation to soften
the reason. To give a vague explanation. To say it's "about fit" when
really there's a specific performance issue. To dress up a hard
conversation as a restructuring.

I understand why people do that — it feels kinder in the moment.

But it isn't. It's kinder to *you*, not to the person being let go.

You owe people a clear reason, not a comfortable excuse.

If someone understands exactly why things didn't work, they have
something to learn from. They can grow. They can do better in the next
role. If all they get is vague language and corporate euphemism, they
leave confused — and often blaming themselves for the wrong things.

The hardest conversations in leadership are the ones where you tell
someone the truth about why things aren't working. But those are also
the most respectful conversations you can have.

## Timing: By the Time You're Certain, You're Usually Late

One more thing about firing: by the time you're absolutely certain,
you're usually late.

Leaders have a tendency to wait for perfect clarity before acting. We
want to be sure. We want to exhaust every option. This is
understandable — it's a big decision, and the consequences for the
person are real.

But what I've seen again and again is this: the moment you start
having serious doubts about whether someone is right for the role is
the moment you should start working on it. Not firing them that week —
but starting the process. Clear expectations. Clear timelines. Clear
support. And a clear point at which, if things haven't changed, the
decision has to be made.

While you wait for absolute certainty, your team is already feeling
the impact. And certainty rarely arrives — it's usually built, slowly,
from the evidence you've been collecting all along.

## The Core Principles

If I had to summarize everything I've learned about this, it would be
a handful of ideas:

- Hire slow. Take the time to find the right person, even when it's
  painful to wait.
- Fire fast — not cruelly, but without pretending the problem will
  solve itself.
- Who you tolerate defines your culture. Silence is a position.
- Hire for character, not comfort. The person who intimidates you a
  little is often the right one.
- Tell people the truth. Clear honesty is more respectful than soft
  euphemism.
- Your best performers are always watching how you handle your worst
  ones.

None of this is easy. Hiring and firing are among the hardest
decisions a leader makes. They involve real people, real livelihoods,
real emotions.

But these decisions shape your team more than any strategy, vision
statement, or motivational talk. Over time, your team becomes the sum
of your hiring and firing decisions — and the spaces in between, where
you decide what you're willing to tolerate.

That, quietly, is the real job.
